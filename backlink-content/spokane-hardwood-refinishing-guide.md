# Hardwood Floor Refinishing in Spokane: Cost Guide, Timing, and What to Expect

Spokane's hardwood floor market is worth knowing before you call anyone. The Inland Northwest's temperature swings — hot, dry summers and cold winters — mean hardwood floors here expand and contract more dramatically than in more temperate climates. That movement, combined with the grit brought in from Spokane's abundant outdoor life, accelerates surface wear. If your floors are looking dull, scratched, or discolored, refinishing is usually the most cost-effective path — but timing and contractor choice matter considerably.

## Spokane's Climate and Hardwood: What Homeowners Need to Know

Wood is hygroscopic — it absorbs and releases moisture in response to ambient humidity. In Spokane's climate, this creates more dramatic seasonal movement than coastal or humid markets:

**Summer (June–September)**: Spokane averages around 25% relative humidity in July — genuinely arid. Wood shrinks and gaps appear between boards. This is normal and not a refinishing problem.

**Winter (November–March)**: Indoor humidity drops further with forced-air heating. Gaps widen. On old floors, this can cause cupping or cracking if the subfloor situation is wrong.

The practical implication: **refinishing is best done in fall or spring** when humidity is transitional and the wood is at middle expansion. Refinishing in peak summer dryness can cause finish adhesion issues; refinishing in a freshly heated winter home on dry wood can cause grain-raising issues.

Hardwood species common in Spokane's older housing stock:
- **Douglas fir**: Found in pre-1970s homes throughout the South Hill, Browne's Addition, and Garland neighborhoods. Soft and shows wear quickly; refinishes differently than hardwoods.
- **Red oak**: Standard in mid-century homes, takes stain predictably.
- **Pine**: Common in historic homes near Downtown and the West Central area. Soft, requires careful sanding technique.
- **Maple**: Popular in 1980s–2000s construction, harder to stain evenly.

## Screen and Recoat vs. Full Sand and Refinish

This distinction significantly affects cost and disruption:

**Screen and recoat** (also called buff and coat): Light abrasion of the existing finish with a buffer, then one or two new finish coats applied. Removes surface scratches and restores sheen. Cannot fix deep scratches, gouging, black water stains, or significant finish wear-through. Takes one day; can walk on in 24 hours.

**Full sand and refinish**: Drum or orbital sanding removes the old finish and a thin layer of wood, then new stain (optional) and 3+ coats of finish applied. Can fix most surface damage. Takes 3–5 days including cure time; furniture stays out longer.

How to tell which you need:
1. Pour a small amount of water on the worn area. If it beads up, the finish is intact — screen and recoat works. If it soaks in immediately, the finish is gone and you need full refinishing.
2. Run your fingernail across the floor. Deep scratches that catch your nail need sanding.
3. Black stains from water or pet accidents cannot be removed without sanding.

## Engineered Hardwood: Can It Be Refinished?

Spokane's housing market has significant engineered hardwood installations from the 1990s–2010s. Whether it can be refinished depends entirely on the wear layer thickness:
- Under 2mm: Cannot refinish — typically one-time use
- 2–3mm: Screen and recoat only
- 3mm+: One careful sand and refinish possible
- 6mm+: Multiple refinishes possible

Look for the product documentation if you have it. Otherwise, an experienced floor contractor can often assess wear layer thickness during an in-home estimate.

## Washington State Contractor Licensing

Washington requires all contractors performing work over $500 to be licensed with the Washington State Department of Labor & Industries (L&I) and bonded. Flooring contractors fall under specialty contractor registration.

Verify any contractor at [verify.lni.wa.gov](https://verify.lni.wa.gov) before signing. This takes 30 seconds and eliminates substantial risk.

## The Dustless Question

Hardwood refinishing generates significant fine wood dust. Traditional sanding creates a dust problem that settles throughout the home. "Dustless" systems use vacuum-equipped sanders that capture 95–98% of dust at the source.

Worth paying the premium for dustless when:
- You have family members with respiratory sensitivities
- The floor is on the second floor or in a space that shares HVAC ducts with living areas
- You can't move out during the refinishing

The finish smell (from oil-based polyurethane specifically) is a separate issue from dust — even dustless jobs require ventilation for 24–48 hours after final coat application. Water-based polyurethane has significantly lower VOC content and dries faster; the tradeoff is a slightly less amber warmth and marginally less durability in high-traffic areas.

## What Questions to Ask Before Hiring

- **What type of finish do you use?** (Water-based vs. oil-based, what brand — Bona, Pallmann, etc.)
- **How many coats?** (Industry standard is 3; less is cutting corners)
- **Do you use dustless equipment?** (Vacuum-equipped vs. traditional)
- **How do you handle stair treads?** (Often additional cost; confirm scope)
- **What's the cure time before furniture goes back?**
- **Do you have references in my neighborhood?** (Browne's Addition old-growth fir is very different from South Hill oak)

## Spokane-Specific Timing Advice

If your floors need refinishing before the holidays or a major event, book in September. Spokane contractors get heavily booked in October and November as homeowners try to complete interior projects before hard winter sets in. Spring (March–April) is the next best window — heating systems are still running but humidity is climbing toward a more stable range for finish application.

For screen and recoat, any time works — it's a faster, less intrusive process with less sensitivity to seasonal conditions.
